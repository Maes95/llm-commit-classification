## Diagnosis

### D1 — No research questions, contributions, or roadmap
The section carried your own two TODOs (`\michel{Add research questions/objectives}`,
`\michel{Add details for next sections}`). Both are now implemented, so the TODO markers were removed.
The rewrite follows the standard structure: context → gap → approach → RQs → findings preview →
contributions → roadmap. Five RQs (RQ1 human ceiling, RQ2 substitutability, RQ3 prompt context,
RQ4 merged labels, RQ5 scale-up) and four contributions.

### D2 — The text contradicted the study design *(most important)*
> "By systematically comparing the automated outputs generated by LLMs against established **ground-truth
> data** produced by human annotators…"

There is no ground truth in this study, and the methodology is built on that fact. The three experts
agree at Krippendorff's α = 0.703 overall and only **0.448 on BPC**; that is precisely why the analysis
uses leave-one-out replacement and the Alt-Test instead of accuracy against a gold set. Claiming a gold
standard in the introduction invites a reviewer to ask why precision/recall are not reported — a question
with no good answer.

The rewrite turns the problem into the paper's hook: manual annotation is expensive *and noisy*, so the
meaningful question is comparative — *is a model as close to the expert consensus as another expert would
be?* — which motivates the choice of metrics before the reader reaches Section III.

### D3 — The annotation task was never defined
A reader finishing the old introduction did not know what is annotated. Nothing ruled out single-label
Conventional-Commits-style classification. The rewrite names BFC/BPC/PRC/NFC, states the five-point
ordinal scale, and says that more than one dimension may be non-zero — and then draws the consequence
explicitly: classification metrics presuppose a single correct label that this task does not have.

### D4 — The motivation targeted the wrong actor
The old framing (developers and maintainers need annotations to understand changes) is the framing that
draws the "if developers needed them they would write them" objection. The rewrite locates the bottleneck
where it actually is in this project: **researchers building annotated corpora** for defect prediction,
bug-seeding, and SZZ-style work, where the cost scales linearly with the corpus and requires subsystem
expertise. Developer metadata is explicitly ruled out as a substitute — `Fixes:` trailers and Conventional
Commits are inconsistent, partial, and record the author's local intent rather than an analyst's
judgment~\cite{11029726}. This also gives the paper an internal argument: the study *strips* `Fixes:`
lines from all messages precisely because they are a shortcut, for humans and models alike.
Release notes, triage, and security auditing are kept as secondary beneficiaries.

### D5 — Zero citations
An IEEE introduction with no references is a red flag. The rewrite cites `11029726` (commit conventions),
`sazid2024commit` and `koyuncu2026exploring` (LLMs for commit/bug-report classification), `wang2025can`
(LLM-as-a-judge), `calderon2025alternative` (Alt-Test), and `baltes2025guidelines` (guidelines followed).
Where no entry exists in `bibliography.bib`, the existing `\cn` macro marks the gap rather than inventing
a reference — see the citation-gap list below.

### D6 — Factual risk: "thousands of daily contributions"
The kernel merges on the order of **200–300 commits per day** (roughly 75–95k per year). "Thousands of
daily contributions" overstates by about an order of magnitude and is exactly the kind of claim a
kernel-literate reviewer checks. The rewrite says "on the order of two hundred commits per day" with a
`\cn` marker; the figure should be pinned to a citable source (Linux Foundation kernel development report,
or a measurement of your own corpus) before submission.

### D7 — The design and its scale were not announced
The old text promised "a rigorous evaluation" without saying what was done. The rewrite states the numbers
that make the study substantial: **eleven models × five prompt configurations × two label spaces = 110
measured configurations**, three agreement metrics, plus the scale-up. It also foregrounds a
differentiator that was previously invisible: **ten of the eleven models are open-weight and were run
locally**, which makes the pipeline cheap, private, and reproducible — a real contrast with studies that
evaluate only hosted frontier models.

### D8 — No findings were previewed
Added, with the verified numbers (notebook re-executed 2026-08-14, §6 of `RESEARCH.md`):

| Claim in the introduction | Evidence |
|---|---|
| No model reaches the human ceiling on four labels | best α = 0.635 (gpt-oss:20b, r3) vs 0.703 baseline; best κ = 0.659 (deepseek-r1:32b, r4) vs 0.721 |
| Merging the bug-related dimensions closes the gap | κ = 0.766 (qwen3-coder:30b, r5) vs 0.751 baseline; several configurations pass the Alt-Test threshold (gpt-oss:120b r3, WR = 0.917) |
| Added context is not monotonically beneficial | diff and few-shot are neutral-to-negative in Study 1; at 908 commits α/κ fall monotonically from r1 to r5 |
| Single-label instruction is over-obeyed | multi-label rate 47.4 % → 0.2–0.4 % vs 20.6–24.3 % among humans |
| Model family beats parameter count | code-specialised models are worst; codellama:70b reaches κ = −0.014, below codellama:34b |

Reporting the negative result first is deliberate: it is the honest headline, and it makes the
merged-label result credible rather than convenient.

### D9 — Overclaiming
"rigorously evaluate", "highly promising technological solutions", "exponential growth" (the last one in
Related Work, where Alexander already flagged it). Replaced with plain, checkable statements.

### D10 — Terminology
"changesets (commonly referred to as commits)" inverts the convention — *commit* is the common term.
"Annotation", "classification", and "categorization" were used interchangeably for what is one specific
task. The rewrite fixes *commit* as the unit, *annotation* as the task, and uses *classification* only
when referring to other people's single-label work.

### D11 — No scope statement
One project, one taxonomy, English commit messages. The rewrite presents this as a deliberate choice
(a deep, expert-annotated baseline is only feasible at this depth), leaving generalisability to threats
to validity. Alexander's comment in `method.tex` (line 24) raises the same concern from the taxonomy side;
that one is still open and is *not* addressed here.

### D12 — The two-study design was absent
Now announced explicitly, with the rationale for the scale-up: testing whether conclusions drawn at
n = 50 survive an eighteen-fold increase in sample size.
